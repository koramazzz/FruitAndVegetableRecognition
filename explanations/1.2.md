# Task 1.2 — Soft-Margin Linear SVM from Scratch

This section presents the results of the custom Soft-Margin Linear SVM implemented using the `cvxopt` quadratic programming solver. The goal was to build the optimization engine manually to understand the internal mechanics of Support Vector Machines, identify support vectors, and analyze the geometry of the decision boundaries.

---

## Performance Overview

The manual implementation successfully solved the optimization problem, achieving performance nearly identical to the library-based approach in Task 1.1.

* **Training Accuracy:** **100.00%**
* **Test Accuracy:** **99.67%**

This confirms that the custom "engine" (the quadratic programming solver) correctly found the optimal weights and biases to separate the fruit categories in the 499-dimensional feature space.

---

## Analyzing the Results

The results tell us a clear story about the "difficulty" and "balance" of the dataset.

#### The "Support Vector" Ratio
* **Total Training Data:** 2400 samples.
* **Total Unique Support Vectors:** 1070.

**Interpretation:**
About **45%** of the data points act as Support Vectors.
-   A **Support Vector** is a data point that "matters." It is either lying exactly on the margin boundary or is slightly on the wrong side (margin violation).
-   The fact that nearly half the dataset is needed to define the boundary suggests that while the classes are separable (high accuracy), the "street" (margin) between them is somewhat crowded. The model has to pay attention to a lot of specific fruit images to maintain that perfect separation.

#### Balanced Difficulty (One-vs-Rest)
Since SVM is a binary classifier, we trained 5 separate "One-vs-Rest" models (e.g., "Is this a Banana or Not?").
The number of support vectors found for each was remarkably consistent:

* **Banana vs. Rest:** 374 SVs
* **Cucumber vs. Rest:** 370 SVs
* **Mandarin vs. Rest:** 371 SVs
* **Potato vs. Rest:** 361 SVs
* **Tomato vs. Rest:** 374 SVs

**Interpretation:**
This consistency proves the dataset is **well-balanced**. No single fruit is drastically harder or easier to identify than the others. The model struggles equally (or succeeds equally) with all categories.

## (a) Visual Inspection: The "Farthest" Points
We identified the data points located farthest away from the decision boundary. These represent the model's most "confident" predictions.

* **The "Positive" Farthest (The Archetype):**
    These are the images the model considers the "perfect" example of that fruit.
    * *Example:* For **Banana**, the most confident positive point (Distance +2.62) is a specific Banana image (Index 657).

* **The "Negative" Farthest (The Opposite):**
    These are the images that look *least* like the target fruit.
    * *Observation:* For **Banana**, **Potato**, and **Tomato**, the "farthest negative" point was often a **Mandarin**.
    * *Interpretation:* Visually, this makes perfect sense. A round, bright orange Mandarin is the complete geometric and color opposite of a long yellow Banana or a red Tomato. The model correctly learned that Mandarins are the "anti-class" for these fruits.

## (b) Pairwise Distances: Who is Confused?
We calculated the Euclidean distance between Support Vectors of different classes to see which categories "rub shoulders" at the boundary.

* **The Closest Pair:** A **Tomato** (Index 817) and a **Cucumber** (Index 1337).
* **Distance:** 11.40 (The smallest gap found).

**Interpretation:**
Even though a human sees Red vs. Green as obvious, in the 499-dimensional feature space (which includes texture and shape), this specific Tomato and Cucumber were the "closest neighbors" across class lines. They define the "danger zone" where the model is most likely to make a mistake.
